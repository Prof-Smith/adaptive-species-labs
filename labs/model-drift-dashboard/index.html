Chapter 9 · Models and Drift

Model Drift Dashboard

A visual forecasting lab showing how a useful model becomes dangerous when the world changes and the user keeps trusting yesterday’s map.

Lab rule: A model is useful because it simplifies the world. A model becomes dangerous when it simplifies away the change that matters.

The Question

When should a decision maker keep trusting a model, update it, widen uncertainty, or abandon it? This lab simulates a model that initially fits the world, then faces noise, regime shifts, and drift.

Chapter 9 connection

Chapter 9 can focus on models as adaptive tools. Models help us act before all the evidence is available, but they must be monitored because environments change.

Guide to the Simulation

The blue line is the changing world. The orange line is the model forecast. The gold band is the model’s uncertainty range. Red vertical markers indicate regime shifts. Students choose whether to trust, update, widen, or replace the model.

True environmentThe blue path the model is trying to track.
Model forecastThe model’s prediction based on its memory of the past.
Uncertainty bandThe range the model admits could plausibly occur.
Regime shiftA structural change that makes old patterns less reliable.
Teaching move: Ask students whether the error is ordinary noise, a warning sign, or evidence that the model’s world has changed.

Reflection Prompts

  • When did the model first become unreliable?
  • Was the error noise or regime change?
  • Did confidence make the model safer or more dangerous?
  • When should the uncertainty band widen?
  • What real-world models are vulnerable to drift?

Chapter Connection

This lab follows the cooperation lab by showing a different kind of adaptation. Chapter 8 asks what happens when other agents adapt back. Chapter 9 asks what happens when the world changes but the model does not.

The danger is not having a model. The danger is forgetting that the model has an environment.

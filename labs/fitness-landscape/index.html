Chapter 4 · Fitness Landscapes

Fitness Landscape Explorer

A visual simulation of adaptation on changing terrain: climb local peaks, explore alternatives, and see why yesterday’s best strategy can become tomorrow’s trap.

Lab rule: Adaptation is not climbing forever. It is knowing when the landscape has changed.

The Question

How does a strategy that once worked become a constraint? This lab turns adaptation into a landscape. Students move an agent across peaks and valleys, compare exploitation with exploration, and watch environmental shocks change which peak is best.

Chapter 4 connection

Chapter 4 can show that success is contextual. A strategy is adaptive only relative to a landscape. When the terrain changes, a well-optimized behavior can become maladaptive.

Guide to the Simulation

The landscape represents strategic fit. High ground means a strategy currently performs well. Low ground means weak fit. The agent follows a search rule: exploit nearby improvements, explore farther options, or balance both. Shocks redraw parts of the terrain.

Local peakA nearby good solution that may not be the best possible solution.
Global peakThe highest currently available strategy, sometimes far away.
ValleyA painful transition zone between old fit and new fit.
ShockA change in the environment that alters what counts as fit.
Teaching move: Ask students whether they are optimizing too early, exploring too randomly, or staying on a peak after the landscape has moved.

How to interpret

High inertia keeps the agent near the old peak. High exploration helps discover distant peaks but may waste movement. High volatility means the map itself changes, so optimization can become fragile.

Reflection Prompts

  • When did exploitation help?
  • When did exploration help?
  • Did the agent get stuck on a local peak?
  • What changed after the environmental shock?
  • What is a real-life example of a strategy that stayed optimized for the wrong landscape?

Chapter Connection

This lab builds on Chapters 1 to 3. After deciding in uncertainty, noticing bias weather, and resisting hindsight, students now see adaptation as movement across changing terrain.

The best strategy is not the one that climbs fastest. It is the one that keeps learning what terrain it is on.

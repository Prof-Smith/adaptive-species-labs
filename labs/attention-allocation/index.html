Chapter 7 · Attention and Adaptive Judgment

Attention Allocation Simulator

A visual lab showing how limited attention gets pulled between signal, noise, salience, urgency, and feedback.

Lab rule: Attention is not just what you notice. It is what your judgment can afford to process.

The Question

How does attention shape judgment before reasoning even begins? This lab treats attention as a scarce resource. Students allocate attention across competing channels and watch accuracy, missed signals, distraction cost, and adaptive learning change.

Chapter 7 connection

Chapter 7 can bridge individual cognition and environmental design. The problem is not simply whether students are careful. The problem is whether the attention environment makes the right signals easier to see.

How to use this lab

1. StartRun the default state and notice the baseline pattern.
2. ChangeMove one control at a time so the cause of the change stays visible.
3. StressUse the stress test, scenario button, or reset to compare worlds.
4. InterpretRead the dashboard before deciding what the result means.
5. TransferUse the reflection prompts to connect the pattern beyond the lab.

Pedagogical spine

Adaptive problemAllocate limited attention among signal, noise, salience, urgency, and feedback.
Misconception challengedBad judgment always begins with bad reasoning.
Transfer targetBefore evaluating a decision, ask what the environment made easy to notice and easy to miss.

How to interpret this lab

The spotlight is the central metaphor. If useful signals sit outside the spotlight while noise or salience sits inside it, attention has been misallocated before reasoning begins.

Quick modeClick Create Overload and ask which useful signals fell outside the spotlight.
Deep modeVary salience, noise, and training quality separately. Compare missed signal and overload risk.

Guide to the Simulation

The map shows a stream of competing signals. Blue items are useful signals. Gray items are noise. Gold items are highly salient but not always useful. Red items are urgent risks. The student’s attention spotlight can cover only part of the environment.

Signal strengthHow much useful information exists in the environment.
NoiseHow much irrelevant information competes for attention.
Salience biasHow often vivid information pulls attention away from useful information.
Feedback delayHow long it takes to learn whether attention was allocated well.
Teaching move: Ask whether poor judgment came from bad reasoning or from the wrong things capturing attention first.

Chapter Connection

This lab follows the narrative trap of Chapter 5. Before people form stories, they first attend. What becomes visible, salient, urgent, or repeated shapes the material from which judgment is built.

Better judgment often begins by changing what the environment makes easy to notice.
